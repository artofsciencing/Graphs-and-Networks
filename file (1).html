Introduction

This lesson introduces the basic language of graph theory before moving to famous problems and applications.

What is a graph?

A graph is a mathematical way of showing connections. A graph is made of vertices (points) and edges (lines joining points).

We often write a graph as G = (V, E), where V is the set of vertices and E is the set of edges.

In this graph, A, B, and C are vertices. The three lines connecting them are edges.

Vertices and edges

Vertex

A vertex is a point in a graph. The plural of vertex is vertices. A vertex can represent a city, a person, a website, a school, or any object.

Edge

An edge joins two vertices. It shows a connection or relationship such as a road, friendship, flight route, or communication link.

Real situation Vertex means Edge means
Road map Town or city Road between places
Social network Person Friendship or follow
Flight network Airport Direct flight
Computer network Device Communication link
Order and size of a graph

The order of a graph is the number of vertices in it.

The size of a graph is the number of edges in it.

Order = number of vertices
Size = number of edges

Example: if a graph has 5 vertices and 7 edges, then its order is 5 and its size is 7.

Degree of a vertex

The degree of a vertex is the number of edges connected to it.

If three edges meet at a vertex, then that vertex has degree 3.

Vertex A is connected to B, C, and D, so the degree of A is 3.

An isolated vertex has degree 0. A pendant vertex has degree 1.

Adjacent vertices and incident edges

Two vertices are adjacent if an edge joins them.

An edge is incident to a vertex if that edge touches the vertex.

In the edge AB, vertices A and B are adjacent, and the edge AB is incident to both A and B.

Subgraphs

A subgraph is a smaller graph formed from some of the vertices and some of the edges of a bigger graph.

Original graph

One subgraph

A subgraph must use only vertices and edges already present in the original graph.

Types of graphs
Simple graph Directed graph Weighted graph Complete graph Connected graph Disconnected graph

Simple graph

No loops and no repeated edges between the same pair of vertices.

Directed graph

Edges have directions, shown by arrows.

Weighted graph

Edges have values such as distance, time, or cost.

Complete graph

Every vertex is joined to every other vertex.

Connected graph

You can move from any vertex to any other by following edges.

Disconnected graph

Some vertices or groups of vertices are not linked to the rest.

Paths, trails, and cycles
  • A walk is any sequence of vertices connected by edges.
  • A trail is a walk with no repeated edges.
  • A path is a walk with no repeated vertices.
  • A cycle is a path that starts and ends at the same vertex.

The Bridges of Königsberg

The city of Königsberg had four land areas connected by seven bridges. People wondered whether it was possible to take a walk that crossed each bridge exactly once.

The problem

Instead of drawing the exact map, Leonhard Euler simplified the situation into a graph.

Each land area became a vertex, and each bridge became an edge.

Euler’s key idea

Euler noticed that a walk using every edge exactly once is possible only in special cases.

For such a walk:

  • Either all vertices have even degree, or
  • Exactly two vertices have odd degree.

In the Königsberg graph, more than two vertices have odd degree, so the walk is impossible.

Why this matters

This problem is often seen as the beginning of graph theory. It showed that the important thing is not the exact shape of the map, but the pattern of connections.

Handshakes and Dating

Graphs can model social connections such as handshakes, friendships, and matching problems.

Handshake graphs

Imagine a party where each person is a vertex. If two people shake hands, draw an edge between them.

The resulting graph shows who has shaken hands with whom.

Sum of degrees

Every edge contributes 2 to the total degree count because each edge touches two vertices.

Sum of all vertex degrees = 2 × number of edges

This is called the handshake theorem.

Odd degree result

In any graph, the number of vertices with odd degree is always even.

This is a very useful result in many graph problems.

Dating and matching

A matching is a set of edges in which no two edges share a vertex.

Matchings are used in pairing students, assigning jobs, or matching applicants to places.

Planar Graphs

A graph is called planar if it can be drawn on a flat surface without any edges crossing.

What planar means

Sometimes a graph appears to have crossings, but by moving vertices around, you can redraw it without crossings. If this is possible, the graph is planar.

Faces and regions

When a planar graph is drawn, it divides the plane into regions called faces. The outside area also counts as a face.

Euler’s formula

For a connected planar graph:

Vertices − Edges + Faces = 2

This is one of the most important formulas in graph theory.

Non-planar graphs

Some graphs can never be drawn without crossings. Two famous examples are:

  • K₅: a complete graph on 5 vertices
  • K₃,₃: a complete bipartite graph with 3 vertices in each group

Map Colouring

Map colouring asks how many colours are needed so that neighbouring regions do not share the same colour.

From maps to graphs

Each region of a map can be represented by a vertex. If two regions share a border, connect the corresponding vertices with an edge.

This turns a map-colouring problem into a graph-colouring problem.

The four-colour theorem

Any flat map can be coloured using at most four colours so that no two adjacent regions have the same colour.

Graph colouring

In graph colouring, we colour vertices so that adjacent vertices have different colours.

This idea is useful in timetabling, scheduling, and resource allocation.

The Travelling Salesman Problem

This problem asks for the shortest route that visits each city once and returns to the starting city.

Graph model

Each city is a vertex. Roads or routes are edges. Distances or travel costs are shown as weights on the edges.

This makes it a weighted graph problem.

Why it is important

The travelling salesman problem appears in delivery routing, circuit design, tour planning, and logistics.

For a small number of cities, the problem is manageable. For large networks, it becomes very difficult.

Scheduling Problems

Graphs can show which tasks must happen before others.

Directed graphs for scheduling

Each task is a vertex. An arrow from A to B means task A must be completed before task B starts.

These are usually shown with directed graphs.

Directed acyclic graphs

A scheduling graph should not contain a cycle, because a cycle would mean tasks depend on one another in a loop.

A directed graph with no cycles is called a directed acyclic graph, or DAG.

Applications
  • School timetables
  • Project planning
  • Manufacturing steps
  • Computer processes

Graphs in Everyday Life

Graph theory is not just about puzzles. It is used in many practical systems.

Common applications

Transport

Stations, bus stops, airports, and routes form graphs.

Internet

Devices and webpages are connected in huge networks.

Social media

Users are vertices, and interactions create edges.

Biology

Food webs, nerve cells, and gene interactions can be studied with graphs.

Computer science

Search engines, networks, and data structures use graph ideas.

Planning

Graphs help solve optimisation and scheduling problems.

Final summary
  • A graph is made of vertices and edges.
  • Order counts vertices; size counts edges.
  • Degree tells how many edges touch a vertex.
  • Subgraphs are smaller graphs from larger ones.
  • Graph theory helps solve problems in maps, travel, planning, and networks.
